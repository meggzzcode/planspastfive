## Research Brief

**Reader.** A woman with a 9 to 5 who wants more friends and more time outside and will organize something small. She is not a hiker and may be shy. She asks: who do I invite, what do I say, what if nobody comes.

**Search intent and long-tail questions** (no volume numbers; intents inferred from the guides read and result titles):
- Informational/how-to: how to start a walking club / walking group; how to start a walking group to make friends; how to find walking buddies; walking group near me.
- Long-tail questions the article answers: how many people do I need; what if only one person shows; how do I invite people without it being awkward; what pace should a social walk be; how do I make a walking group welcoming to all abilities; what should be in the invitation; what to do in bad weather; how to keep a walking group going; how long does it take to make a friend as an adult; is it safe to meet a walking group from an online page.
- Informational to be wary of: "walking group near me" is a local/directory intent the article cannot serve; it only describes finding or starting one.

**What the top guides cover and what they leave unanswered** (read: Toronto, Washington State dementia-friendly guide, CDC-funded Partnership for Prevention action guide, UK Travelwest, Yahoo Monday Mile piece; AARP, AHA and Ramblers pages were blocked or empty, see Sources):
- They are written for organizations, health programs or workplaces (waivers, trained leaders, evaluation plans, release forms). They assume a program, not a friend.
- They treat the group as an exercise-adherence tool. None answers "how do I make friends this way" with an honest time estimate.
- They rarely give copyable words: an invitation message, a first-walk script, what to say at the start.
- Few address the failure modes of a tiny group: only one person shows, group chat noise, who cancels for weather, what happens after the first month.
- Accessibility appears in the dementia-friendly and Toronto guides but as a program checklist; nothing translates it into an invitation line for a casual club (terrain, restrooms, two distances, step-free coffee).
- Gap this article fills: a small, informal organizer's guide with scripts, a rotating-leader and group-chat rule, coffee as the friendship engine tied to a real source (Hall), and honest limits.

**Angle.** Start tiny, copy-paste words, one fixed slot, a route people can talk on, then rules that keep it alive. The only "why it works" claim is sourced to Hall and hedged; no mental-health or friendship promises.

## Open uncertainties

- Ramblers wellbeing-walk guidance (the most relevant walking-group organization) could not be read; trial-walk advice is in the article as plain advice with no source claim.
- Hall's numbers are survey averages from recent movers and students; the study is not about walking. The article says so. The KU article date is inconsistent (March 6 in URL vs March 28 in text).
- "Ninety minutes a week" and "48 weeks" are our assumptions, not data; the claim is marked as our arithmetic.
- "Meet first in a public place in daylight and tell a friend" for people met through a community page is common-sense editorial advice, not tied to a source I read.
- Spelling: the revised draft is US; the older published articles mix UK forms, so the site convention is still the editor's call.
- Unsourced editorial advice in the revised draft (not tied to a page read): walk the route once first; meet new contacts in a public place in daylight; a rain plan of meeting for coffee anyway (the Washington guide's partial-cancellation idea is similar); the follow-up-note wording; the "talks too much" and "says no" scripts; indoor loop at a mall or community center in winter (the Partnership guide names malls and indoor tracks as weather alternatives).
- The second-activity ideas (picnic, craft night, dinner) are suggestions, not sourced claims.
- Local specifics (sunset times, park rules) vary by town; the revised article no longer makes any claim about waivers, permits or insurance.
- No peer-reviewed evidence read that walking groups themselves create friendships; the article makes no such claim.
