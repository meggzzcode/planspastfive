Picture it at 6:25 on a Friday. The lamp is on, four racks sit at four seats, and the first knock on the door is about to land. By 9:30 your friends will have said "wait, how does this one work" at least twice, and the snacks will have stayed off the tiles. That is a mahjong night, and it is easier to host than it looks.

One note on scope. This guide is about **American mahjong**, the version played with a card of hands that the National Mah Jongg League publishes every year. Other families of mahjong, such as Chinese, Japanese or international rules, use different tiles and different hands, so if a guest learned another version, tell her in the invitation which one tonight is.

## A realistic timeline for four

Mahjong is not a game you can rush, and a first night is slower than later ones. This schedule assumes four players, three hours and at least one beginner. These timings are our suggested pacing for hosting, not a rule of the game. Move the clock, keep the order.

- **6:30** Guests arrive. Drinks first, mahjong talk later.
- **6:50** Sit down. Pass the tiles around so people can see and touch them.
- **7:00** Teach the shape of the game, ten minutes at most (see below).
- **7:15** Walk through one hand slowly, out loud, with everyone watching.
- **7:45** Play a first real hand, slowly. Questions are welcome.
- **8:15** Break. Refill drinks, wash hands, bring out the second round of snacks.
- **8:30** Two or three more hands, with the table starting to run itself.
- **9:15** Announce the last hand, so the evening has an ending.
- **9:30** Tiles back in their case. Goodbyes.

Protect the 7:15 walk-through for anyone brand new. It decides whether she comes back.

## Setup and seating

Put something soft under the tiles. A felt cloth, a folded blanket or a thick tablecloth stops the clatter and keeps tiles from skating. At each seat, put a rack, a card and a coaster or small napkin. Nothing else. Phones go face-down on the side table.

Keep food and drink on that side table, fetched as needed and never set down among the tiles. Say it once, kindly, at 6:50: "Snacks live over there, and hands get wiped before we pick up a tile."

## Welcoming beginners: a short teaching flow

Most hosts over-teach. The goal at 7:00 is not to explain mahjong. It is to get four people to the point where playing a hand is easier than discussing one. The League describes the game as one played against a card of standard hands that changes every year, and it says plainly that only a small amount of effort is needed to become familiar with the basics. Here is a flow that keeps the lecture short.

1. **Pass the tiles around.** Name the three suits, the winds, the dragons, the flowers and the jokers. Jokers are the tiles that make beginners smile. They are wild for most groups of three or more, but never for a pair or a single, and the card's notes say where they apply.
2. **Show the card.** Every hand on it is a pattern, and the goal is to build one of them from the tiles you draw and the tiles you pick up.
3. **Do the Charleston lightly.** The game opens with the Charleston, in which players pass three unwanted tiles to one another. The first round goes right, then across, then left, and jokers are never passed. A second round happens only if every player agrees, so on a first night it is fine to say no.
4. **Play one hand face up.** You, slowly, naming each choice: "I am keeping this pair because it fits this line." Beginners learn more from watching a plan than from hearing rules.
5. **Play one for real.** No scoring talk. Check the card and the League's instructions after the hand, not during it.

When someone is lost, a useful sentence is: "Pick one line on the card and hold the tiles that fit it." A player normally holds 13 tiles, and 14 on her own turn, so counting is a handy first check when something feels off.

For the full rules, use the instruction book that comes with a League set, a local teacher, or a friend who plays. A host does not have to be an expert, only a few pages ahead.

## Snacks and drinks

Think bite-sized and not greasy: things you can eat with one hand and a napkin. Oily fingers are hard on paper cards.

- Cheese cubes with picks, crackers or breadsticks
- Grapes, clementine segments, apple slices with a little lemon
- Roasted nuts or chickpeas in a bowl with a spoon
- Shortbread or dark chocolate squares

For drinks, lead with tea, coffee, sparkling water and a jug of lemonade or iced tea. If guests would like wine, let it be an extra rather than the centrepiece. Counting and card-reading take attention, and a beginner needs hers. Lidded cups with coasters stay on the side table.

## House etiquette, in five lines

- The card decides. Check it after the hand.
- Keep your tiles to yourself, and no commentary on another rack.
- Announce a discard out loud so the table hears it.
- A beginner may ask the host for help. Everyone else waits to be asked.
- Mistakes are normal tonight. Fix it and carry on.

## What beginners get wrong

- **Starting without the card.** Nobody remembers the hands. Keep a card in front of each player for the whole evening.
- **Using an old card.** The hands change every year, so an old card quietly teaches the wrong ones.
- **Teaching too much too soon.** Four rules beat twenty.
- **Letting the evening drift.** Name a last hand.

## Borrow or buy

If a friend owns a set, borrowing it is the easiest start. If you are buying, the list is short.

**Tiles.** Look for a set made for American mahjong, and read the maker's own page. A standard American set uses 152 tiles, including eight jokers and eight flowers, according to the maker Yellow Mountain Imports, and some makers add spare tiles beyond that, so a count above 152 is not unusual. Check that the listing states its counts rather than leaving you to guess. What to look for in a first set:

- **Racks in the box.** Four racks, one per seat. Tile size varies between makers, so racks bought with the tiles are the safest match.
- **Pushers.** Some racks include them, some sell them separately. Check which.
- **A case.** A soft case or a hard one with a handle makes the set easy to bring to someone else's table.
- **What the maker says is included.** Dice, scoring coins and an instruction book vary from set to set.

## Questions

**Do I need to know the game before I host?**
No. You need to set up the table, explain the aim and follow the card. Being a few pages ahead of your guests is a fine place to start.

**Which year's card should we use?**
The current one. The League changes its card annually, so use this year's, and look for next year's on the League's website.

**What if a guest plays a different version?**
Say in the invitation that tonight is American mahjong with the League's card. The other version is welcome at another table, on another night.

## Send the invitation

Pick a date, send the invitation on Monday with the words "American mahjong, beginners welcome", and put the side table where the snacks will go. The rest you can borrow.
