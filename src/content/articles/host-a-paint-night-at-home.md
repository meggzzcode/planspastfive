Every paint night has one moment. Around 7:15, someone leans back from her canvas and says, "I did warn you, I cannot paint." Then she looks again, and the blue she just laid down is rather good, and she stops apologising. A host cannot make that happen, but she can set the evening up so it has room to.

This guide is about hosting, not choosing a kit. It is for two to six friends around one table, nobody expected to have held a brush since school. The order is the order you will do things: invite, settle who pays, set the table, run the evening, send everyone home.

## Start with the invitation

Etiquette guidance from the Emily Post Institute says an invitation should carry the date, time, place and how to reply, plus anything guests need to know, such as what to wear. For a paint night that means three plain facts: no skill needed, wear something you do not mind marking, and there are alcohol-free drinks. Send it about a week ahead. Here is a message to copy:

> Paint night at mine, Friday at 6:30. There will be a small canvas and everything you need at your seat, and no painting skill required. Wear something you do not mind marking. I will have snacks and plenty of drinks without alcohol. If the date falls through we will move it a week, so tell me early.

That last line is your reschedule plan, and it works better said up front than invented on the day. If it falls through, reschedule by a week and keep the pot lids closed.

## Decide who pays, and count honestly

Guests should not meet a surprise bill. Pick one of these before you send the invitation:

- **You cover it**, the way you would a dinner.
- **Everyone chips in** for her own kit, and the invitation says so.
- **A shared pile.** Each guest brings a canvas or paints she already owns, and you supply brushes and water. This is the cheapest route, and it suits friends who already paint a little.

A kit for every painter is a real step up from a shared pile, and you count as a painter too. Here is the arithmetic. Quantities beyond kits and seats are our own estimates.

| Per guest | 2 painters | 4 painters | 6 painters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kits (one each, host included) | 2 | 4 | 6 |
| Seats and water cups | 2 | 4 | 6 |
| Paper towels | 1 roll to share | 1 roll | 2 rolls, one at each end |
| Table | a small table | one dining table | two tables pushed together |
| Snack plates | 2 | 4 | 6 |

Six painters means five friends and you. A home-party guide found a 5 x 3 ft dining table held about six easels, which is the tightest you want to go.

## One project or free choice

Everyone paints the same guided project, or everyone paints what she likes. Avoid mixing them. A numbered guide or traceable template gives a nervous guest a place to start. Free choice is lovely for a group that already paints, and it can leave a beginner facing a blank canvas, so give her a simple subject, such as a bowl of lemons, and say nothing is graded. Whichever you pick, paint the project once beforehand so you know where people stumble.

## Set the table

Cover it with something you can fold up wet or throw away: an old flat sheet, a shower curtain liner, kraft paper. Ask guests to wear old shirts, and keep a spare by the door. At each place put a canvas, a cup of water, a paper towel and a paper plate for mixing. Tie a ribbon on the water cup so nobody mistakes it for a drink.

Light helps more than you would expect. Use a window while it is bright, then lamps at both ends, not one overhead bulb that throws every hand into shadow. Keep any candle far from sheets and paper. Make a playlist beforehand, low enough to talk over, and skip anything with lyrics that pulls people out of a sentence. Seat the most nervous guest beside the friend who will cheer her on.

## The run of show

Two and a half hours, the same as the "craft and paint nights" entry in [our hobbies guide](/journal/hobbies-for-busy-women/). It is our suggested pacing, so move it around.

| Time | You do | Guests do |
|---|---|---|
| 6:15 | Be ready before anyone knocks | Nothing yet |
| 6:30 | Greet each guest, hand her a drink | Settle in, nibble, talk |
| 7:00 | Show your practice picture, say "this is about the evening" | Start with the biggest areas |
| 7:15 | Check in once with each painter | Paint, chat, hear the "I can't" moment |
| 7:45 | Brushes into water, hand-washing, snacks out | Break, wash hands, eat |
| 8:00 | Refill drinks, then step back | Back to the canvases in short stretches |
| 8:30 | Take the photo | Hold up what you have so far |
| 8:45 | Start the wipe-down | Help for ten minutes |
| 9:00 | Thank everyone at the door | Take canvases home to finish |

Hold up what you have so far is the reveal, and it is a half-finished one. A kit takes longer than one evening, so the first night is a start. Acrylic also needs touch-dry time. Winsor & Newton gives about 10 to 20 minutes for thin Galeria coats and an hour or more for thick ones, and it says conditions vary, so do not promise a wet canvas is ready to carry.

## When a guest says she can't paint

You will hear it. A short script that works:

1. "Nothing here is graded, and nobody is judging anyone's picture."
2. "Start with the biggest shape, the sky or the water, and ignore the details."
3. "A wrong colour is now a different picture."

Then leave her to it. If she finishes early, hand her a second canvas, or ask her to help a friend pick a colour. If she is much slower, nobody waits, and the break happens on time regardless.

## Snacks and drinks that stay off the paint

Keep food and drink on a side table and bring it out at the break, as at a [mahjong night](/journal/how-to-host-a-mahjong-night/). Choose things that will not stain if tipped: crackers, grapes, pretzels, shortbread, chocolate. For anything perishable, such as cheese cubes, put out a small plate and refill it, because Penn State Extension says not to leave food without temperature control for more than two hours. Everyone washes her hands before eating, and brushes live in water, not in mouths, as Liquitex advises acrylic painters.

Treat non-drinkers as guests, not afterthoughts. The NIAAA advises real alcohol-free options such as water, juice and sparkling drinks, more effort on the food than the bar, labelling what contains alcohol, and never making a guest explain a "no thanks." Sparkling water with fruit, iced tea and lemonade do it. Wine is the extra.

## Cleanup and what goes home

1. Wipe brushes and mixing plates with a paper towel, then wash them with mild soap and cool water straight away. Winsor & Newton says dried acrylic is insoluble, so do not let paint dry on a brush.
2. Keep paint sludge and dirty wipes out of the sink, in a waste bin you can seal (Liquitex). Golden notes that acrylic solids can clog drains, and the Washington State Department of Health advises asking your local health department if you are unsure.
3. Close every pot lid firmly.
4. Roll up the sheet with the paint side in, and wipe the table.
5. Send wet or unfinished canvases home flat in a box or tote, or keep a wet one overnight and hand it over tomorrow.

If this goes well, the same table suits [air-dry clay](/journal/air-dry-clay-for-beginners/) or a [junk journal spread](/journal/your-first-junk-journal-spread/) next time.

## After the last goodbye

Roll the sheet up with the paint side in, carry it to the porch and turn off the lamp at the far end. On the sideboard sits one small canvas nobody collected, a pond of uneven blues with a name pencilled on the back. Text its owner, "kept safe, finish it whenever," and you have your next paint night half planned.
