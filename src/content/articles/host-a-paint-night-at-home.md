Picture the table at 6:25. An old flat sheet is smoothed over it, a lamp is pulled close, and four small canvases sit at four seats, each with a cup of water and a folded paper towel. The playlist is low. By 7:00 someone will say "I can't paint, I should warn you," and by 8:30 the same person will be holding her picture up to the light, delighted. That is the evening this guide is for.

It is written for a host with two to six friends and no art teacher. Nobody needs painting skill. What the night needs is a plan, a table that can take a spill, and a few decisions made before the doorbell.

## Choose the shape of the night first

There are two honest ways to run it, and mixing them is where evenings go wrong.

**Everyone paints the same guided project.** Each guest gets the same kit or the same simple template, and the evening has a shared rhythm. Painting from a template or a numbered guide is the most forgiving option for people who have not touched a brush since school, and several hosting guides say the same: a template means a guest can paint with confidence from the first stroke. This is our recommendation for a first paint night, and the one the rest of this guide assumes.

**Everyone paints whatever she likes.** It is lovely for a group that already paints, and it leaves a nervous guest staring at a blank canvas. If you go this way, put a few simple photos or objects on the table, such as a bowl of lemons, and say out loud that nobody is graded.

Whichever you pick, paint the project once yourself beforehand. Several of the guides we read give the same advice, and it is how you know where people will get stuck.

## How many people, and how much table

Count the painters, not the guests: you paint too, so four friends means five kits. The room, not the guest list, sets the limit. One home-party guide says a 5 by 3 foot dining table fit about six easels, and that was with everyone working on larger canvases. A six-friend evening means seven painters, so plan two tables pushed together or a table plus a counter. Whatever the count, give each person elbow room and her own water cup.

## A realistic timeline

This plan runs two and a half hours, which matches the "about 2.5 hours" we give craft and paint nights in our [hobbies guide](/journal/hobbies-for-busy-women/). It is our suggested pacing, not a rule. One host guide advises starting before 7 p.m. so guests are not worn out after roughly two hours of painting, and another says tutorials usually run one to two hours before you add time for pauses and chatting.

- **6:30** Guests arrive. Drinks and a nibble first, no art talk yet.
- **6:50** Sit down. Show the finished example you painted, and say the line that matters: "This is about the evening, not the picture."
- **7:00** Paint. Start with the biggest shapes and the easiest colours, with the music low enough to talk over.
- **7:40** Break. Brushes into the water cup, hands washed, snacks come out. Stand up and walk around the table.
- **8:00** Paint again. Let the group set its own pace; one guide puts it neatly as "art doesn't wear a wristwatch."
- **8:30** Reveal. Everyone holds hers up, and the only comment allowed is something kind.
- **8:45** Wipe-down and packing up, with everyone helping for ten minutes.
- **9:00** Goodbyes.

Painting first and snacking later is a good rule. One guide suggests starting with the painting and moving guests to the food while canvases dry, so nobody leaves holding a wet picture.

## Set the table

Cover it. Most of us already own what works: an old flat sheet, a shower curtain liner, a roll of kraft paper or a plastic tablecloth from a past party. Hosting guides recommend drop cloths or disposable cloths, and one calls them a lifesaver in someone else's home. Ask guests to wear something they do not mind marking, and keep a few old shirts by the door for anyone who forgets. Aprons are nice but not needed.

At each seat, set one canvas, one cup of water, a paper towel, and a paper plate as a palette if your project needs one. Tie a ribbon or pick a distinctive cup for the water so nobody mistakes it for a drink. One host guide does exactly that, and it is a fun trick for a night with glasses on the table.

Light matters more than you would think. Use a window if it is still light and add lamps at each end of the table, not a single overhead bulb that throws every hand into shadow. Fairy lights or candles are pretty, but keep any flame well away from paper towels and sheets. Choose a playlist that needs no skipping and stays low enough to talk over.

Seat the most nervous guest beside someone who will cheer her on.

## Snacks and drinks that stay off the paint

The rule is that nothing wet or greasy sits near the canvases. Keep food and drinks on a side table and bring them out at the break, as you would at a [mahjong night](/journal/how-to-host-a-mahjong-night/). Choose things that will not stain if tipped, which means going easy on red wine, beetroot dip and bright sauces. Good choices are cheese cubes, crackers, grapes, pretzels, shortbread and chocolate squares, or anything you can eat in two bites with a napkin.

For drinks, remember the guest who is not drinking tonight. The NIAAA's advice for hosting is to offer real alcohol-free choices such as water, juice and sparkling drinks, to spend more effort on the food than on the bar, to label what contains alcohol, and never to make a guest explain a "no thanks." A pitcher of sparkling water with fruit, iced tea and a jug of lemonade, set out as the main drinks rather than an afterthought, covers it. If you serve wine, it is the extra.

Paint safety is simple. Painters should wash their hands before they eat or drink, and a brush goes in the water cup, not the mouth. Liquitex says exactly that in its own guidance for acrylic painters. In practice: break time begins with the hand-washing queue.

## Keeping it low-pressure for the one who can't paint

Most nerves are cured by the host's first three sentences. Try them: "There is no wrong colour tonight. If a bit goes wrong, it is now an abstract. The goal is an evening, and the picture is a bonus."

Some other things that help:

- Show your own practice painting, wobbles included, rather than a perfect one.
- Start with the largest area, and tell people the small details can wait for the end.
- Compliment the choices, not the skill: "I love how bold that blue is."
- Check in once, then leave her to it.

## Cleanup without a project

Wet acrylic is easiest to deal with while it is still wet, so do the cleanup within the last half hour. Liquitex's advice is to wipe brushes, palettes and tools with a paper towel before washing, and to keep leftover paint and paint-sludge out of the sink, using a dedicated container for waste paint and dirty wipes. The Washington State Department of Health advises against pouring hazardous art wastewater down the drain and says to ask the local health department if you are unsure. With small kit pots there is very little left over, but the habit is a good one: wipe first, rinse second, and check local rules for rinse water.

Roll the sheet up with the paint side in. Wipe the table, wash the cups, and leave the brushes to dry. Do not leave a brush with wet paint overnight.

## What to do with the finished pieces

Let the pictures sit flat on the table or an uncluttered counter until you say goodbye, and send them home in a clean tote or box. If one still feels wet to the touch, keep it overnight and hand it over the next day, rather than risking a smudged seat in someone's car. The easel included in the Monet kit makes a finished picture easy to stand on a shelf, and the kit has a hang tab. A photo of everyone holding theirs, taken after the reveal, is the best souvenir. ## Borrow or buy

The night works without any product on this page. Friends who already own acrylics and canvases can run a free-choice evening from a shared pile. A kit earns its place when you want the whole group painting the same picture without anyone planning a lesson, and when it is easier to buy seven identical sets than to collect supplies. Order at least a week ahead, since one guide notes that kits take time to arrive.

## Questions

**How many guests is too many?**
Seven painters at one dining table is a stretch. Use two tables or limit it to five or six.

**Do I need to know how to paint?**
No. You need to try the project yourself first and run the clock.

**What if someone cancels or it rains?**
Paint night is indoors, so weather matters only for getting there. Offer a rain-check in the invitation: "If a plan falls through, we move it a week, same kits." Painting from kits is easy to postpone because the kit keeps.

## Send the invitation

Here is a message you can copy:

> Paint night at mine, Friday at 6:30! I will have a small canvas and everything you need at your seat. No painting skill required, and no one will see your picture but us. Wear something you do not mind marking. Dinner is snacks and a few drinks, with plenty that are alcohol-free. If the date falls through, we will rain-check for a week later. Reply by Wednesday so I know how many seats to set.

Send it a week ahead, count kits as soon as the replies arrive, and set the table the night before. The rest is a lamp, a playlist and a spare sheet.
