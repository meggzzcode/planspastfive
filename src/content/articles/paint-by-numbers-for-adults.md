Picture a small painting propped on a bookshelf, next to a plant and a stack of paperbacks. Soft blue water, a few pink lilies, and a brushy, Impressionist look that nobody would guess was done by following numbers. You painted it over a few evenings with a lamp on and one good playlist. When a friend says "Where did you get that?", the answer is "I made it."

That is the promise of paint by numbers, and it is only kept by some kits. This guide is about telling them apart before you buy.

## What it actually is

A paint-by-numbers kit gives you a picture already divided into small numbered areas, plus paints that carry matching numbers. You fill each area with its colour. Nobody has to draw anything, which is the point: the composition is decided, and you supply the patience.

The idea is older than the craft aisle. Detroit commercial artist Dan Robbins helped develop the kits for the Palmer Paint Company in the early 1950s, and in his memoir he recalled borrowing the numbered-sections idea from Leonardo da Vinci's way of guiding apprentices. The kits were sold as a way for anyone to have the experience of making a painting. Critics argued about whether it counted as art. For your purposes it does not need to: you want a calm evening and a picture you like.

## How long does it take?

It varies, and no honest number fits every kit. Retailers' own estimates range widely, and the main drivers are the size of the canvas, how many colours there are, how small the numbered areas are, and how long you wait between coats. One kit maker's guidance is that a thin layer is touch-dry in roughly 10 to 20 minutes, with at least an hour before a second coat or painting right up against a fresh edge. That is why most kits are a few evenings rather than one.

So treat the first evening as a start. A single evening is enough to begin, to get the hang of the brush, and to finish the first colours. Finishing in one sitting is not the goal.

## What makes a kit worth displaying

Look at these six things, roughly in this order.

**The picture.** This is the whole game. Choose a scene you would hang if it were already framed, in colours that suit your room.

**How fine the numbered areas are.** Reviewers of kits with many colours complain most about tiny sections and numbers that are hard to read, and about numbers that look alike (one reviewer said the 30s look like 20s). One guide suggests avoiding designs with more than 30 colours for a first attempt. Fewer, larger areas are kinder to tired eyes after work, and simple designs finish sooner. If you love detail, a magnifying glass helps, and a toothpick tip can dot very small areas.

**Canvas size and quality.** Bigger means more time and more tiny shapes. Smaller means a faster, more manageable finish. A canvas board or pre-stretched canvas lies flat. A rolled canvas may need flattening before you start.

**Paint quantity and type.** Acrylic is the standard, dries fast and cleans up with water. Check that paint is premixed, so every colour you need is in the box. The catch with a small number of pots is a simplified picture, which is fine if the design was drawn for it.

**Brushes.** Having at least one brush in the box matters more than having a drawer of them. Smaller brushes help with detail, so a kit that ships only one wide brush will ask more patience of you.

**Easel and frame.** If the kit includes a display easel or a frame, the finished painting can go on show the same night it dries. Otherwise you are shopping for a frame later, which is where many projects stall.

## Mistakes beginners make, and the quick fixes

Most of these come up in several kit makers' guides.

- **Too much paint on the brush.** Dip only the tip and wipe off the excess. A loaded brush bleeds into the next area and fills in small shapes.
- **Paint that is too thin or too thick.** A soaking-wet brush leaves weak, streaky colour. If paint thickens, add a drop of water at a time and stir.
- **Numbers showing through light colours.** Let the first coat dry, then add a second thin coat rather than one heavy one.
- **Smudging.** Start at the top and work down and away from your painting hand, and let a section dry before painting against it.
- **Painting over the numbers you still need.** Photograph the numbered canvas before you start. Then you can check which colour goes where after the numbers disappear.
- **Leaving the pots open.** Acrylic dries quickly. Close each lid, and rinse brushes when you change colours.
- **A wrong colour.** Let it dry completely, then paint over it with the correct one.

One honest disagreement: guides split on order. Some say light before dark so dark never has to cover light, and others say dark first because it hides the printed numbers better. If your kit's instructions give an order, follow it. If not, pick one approach and stay with it.

## What to skip

- **A set of extra brushes.** Use what is in the box first. Buy a finer brush only if the numbered areas are too small for the included one.
- **Varnish, at least at first.** It is optional. Makers of acrylic paint describe it as a way to even out the sheen, keep dust off and add some UV protection, but it is not needed to finish the picture. If you do want it later, wait until the paint is fully dry (Liquitex says that can take up to two weeks) and use thin coats.
- **A kit with a very long colour list.** One guide suggests keeping a first kit under about 30 colours. More colours mean more tiny shapes, which is a long project for a tired Thursday.

## Where kits disappoint

Reviews of kits from other makers point to the same weak spots: very small sections, numbers that are hard to read, paints that run short, a missing frame or instructions, and paint colours that do not match the picture on the box. A bigger frame in the box does not fix a design that is too busy. Check the listing for what is included and the canvas size, because packaging dimensions sometimes get listed in place of canvas dimensions.

## Finishing and displaying

Step back and look at the painting from a couple of feet away. Touch up missed spots, and tidy any rough edges with a small brush. Let it dry. Prop it on the easel that came in the box, or hang it if the kit has a hang tab or frame. A shelf, a sideboard or a corner of your desk will do. It does not need a wall.

## Your next step

Choose by the picture, then by the size. Order one kit, photograph the numbered canvas, and give yourself a first evening to paint just two or three colours. That is enough to find out if you like it.
