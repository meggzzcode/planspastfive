Picture a small dish on your dresser. It is the colour of cream, with a scalloped edge and one cherry-red rim, and it holds your rings, a spare hair clip and the earring that has lost its partner. You made it on a Thursday evening, at the kitchen table, with a block of clay and a butter knife.

That is air-dry clay. It hardens in the open air, so there is no kiln, no oven and no studio. You press, pinch, smooth, wait a day or two, and paint. Your first piece will be a little lopsided, and that is its charm.

## Three little things to make

**The cherry-rim ring dish.** A pinched, shallow bowl in matte white, no bigger than your palm, with one hand-painted cherry-red edge. It lives by the bathroom sink or the bed.

**The bow-and-scallop trinket dish.** A flat, round dish with a frilled edge, or a soft bow shape cut from a paper template, in blush with a butter-yellow rim. A good one for a friend's birthday.

**The coil-lip tray.** A small oval with a low rolled edge and a pattern pressed into the base, painted in olive or sage. It holds earrings, keys and the odd button.

## One honest note

These pieces are **decorative only**. Air-dry clay is not food-safe, and it is not waterproof. Keep it for trinkets, not for snacks, drinks or flowers in water.

## What you need

Clay, paint and, if you like, a clear finish. The rest is in your kitchen drawer. Both picks below also live in [the Shop's creative shelf](/shop/#creative).

**Clay.** Look for a white, air-hardening clay from a maker that explains drying, painting and cracks. White takes soft colours best. The tray takes about half a kilo on its own, and the two small dishes need roughly the rest, so a 1 kg block covers all three if you keep them small. **A finish (optional).** Look for a matte, water-based clay sealer from the clay's own maker, brushed on with a soft, wide synthetic brush in a ventilated room. It protects the surface but does not turn a dish into tableware. Crayola says sealing will not waterproof clay, so keep every piece dry either way, and try the sealer on a spare scrap first.

**From the kitchen drawer:** a rolling pin or straight-sided bottle, a butter knife and a toothpick, a lid or paper template to cut around, baking parchment or cling film, a small bowl of water and a sponge, fine sandpaper, and a soft brush. A scalloped cutter is a nice extra, but a template and a butter knife make the same shape.

## The one rule: even thickness

Keep each piece the same thickness all over. Makers differ on the minimum: Mont Marte rolls its tray to 1 cm, Crayola says at least 1/4 inch, and Sculpd says at least 1/2 inch in all places. Follow the pack of the clay you buy. Makers also name too much water and uneven drying as the usual causes of cracks.

## Project 1: pinch-pot ring dish

1. Roll a ball of clay about the size of a small plum.
2. Press your thumb into the middle, stopping before the bottom.
3. Pinch slowly around, turning as you go, until the walls are even and the dish is shallow.
4. Smooth cracks with a barely damp fingertip.
5. Set it on parchment to dry. Once dry, paint it white, then add a cherry-red rim.

## Project 2: scalloped or bow trinket dish

1. Roll the clay between two sheets of cling film to an even thickness.
2. Cut a circle around a lid. For scallops, press a small round lid or bottle cap into the edge, overlapping the dents all the way round. Or cut a bow or heart from a paper template.
3. Lift the shape and pinch or roll the edge upward into a shallow curve.
4. Smooth the edge with a damp fingertip and leave to dry. Paint it blush with a butter-yellow rim.

## Project 3: coil-lip tray

1. Roll about 500 g to an even slab.
2. Cut an oval, roughly 22 x 12 cm, around a paper template.
3. Score a line about 1 cm inside the edge. Press a thin coil of clay onto it for a low lip, and blend the join with a damp fingertip.
4. Press a pattern into the base with a toothpick or the back of a spoon.
5. Leave to dry.

Mont Marte publishes a free trinket-dish project for its own clay, a good place to see this shape made.

## Drying, painting and finishing

Dry each piece somewhere cool and quiet, away from radiators and direct sun. Do not use the oven: Sculpd says not to apply heat to its clay. Makers' own timings:

- **Mont Marte:** about 24 hours to reach earthenware hardness, longer for thicker pieces. For its tray, 24 to 48 hours before painting.
- **Crayola:** about 24 hours dry to the touch and 72 hours to dry completely, depending on conditions and size.
- **Sculpd:** 24 to 48 hours, longer for thicker pieces.

So the honest answer is a day or two, maybe longer, and the instructions on your clay's pack win. When a piece is completely dry, sand any rough edge lightly, wipe off the dust and paint. If you seal, Mont Marte advises waiting at least 24 to 72 hours after painting, then two or three even coats.

## When it goes a bit wrong

- **A crack.** Press fresh clay into it, smooth it with a barely damp finger and dry it slowly.
- **A rough edge.** Wait until it is completely dry, then sand lightly.
- **Tacky paint.** Thick paint takes longer to dry than you expect. Wait a day, or use thinner coats next time.

## Quick questions

**Do I need an oven or a kiln?** No. Sculpd specifically says not to put its clay in the oven.

**What can I put in it?** Rings, earrings and keys. Not water: Sculpd says its clay is porous and can absorb water even after drying. Mont Marte says plainly that air-dry clay is not food-safe.

**Do I have to seal it?** No. A finish helps protect the surface and paint, but it will not make clay waterproof or food-safe.

## Your next step

Pick up a block of white clay and a few acrylics this week. Make the ring dish on Sunday, leave it on the windowsill, and paint it when it is dry. By midweek you will have something small, pretty and entirely yours.
