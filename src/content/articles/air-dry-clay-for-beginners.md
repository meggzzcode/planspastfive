Picture a small dish on your dresser. It is the colour of cream, with a scalloped edge and one cherry-red rim, and it holds your rings, a spare hair clip and the earring that has lost its partner. You made it on a Thursday evening, at the kitchen table, with a block of clay and a butter knife.

That is air-dry clay. It hardens in the open air, so there is no kiln, no oven and no studio. You press, pinch and smooth, you leave it alone for a bit, and then you paint it. Your first piece will be a little lopsided. That is its charm, and it still looks good on a dresser.

## Why it is a good first hobby

It is forgiving. A wobbly edge can be smoothed with a damp fingertip, a dent can be filled with a little more clay, and a thumbprint becomes a feature. It is also quiet, tidy and small. A ring dish fits on one end of the table, and nothing needs plugging in.

Best of all, the finished piece is useful. Rings, keys and stray earrings all need somewhere to sit, and now they have a place you made yourself.

One honest note: these pieces are **decorative only**. Air-dry clay is not food-safe, and it is not waterproof. Keep it for trinkets, not for snacks, drinks or flowers in water.

## What you need

Three things, and they should agree with each other: a clay, a set of acrylic paints and, if you like, a clear finish. The rest is in your kitchen drawer.

**Clay.** Look for a white, air-hardening clay from a maker that explains how to use it: how long it takes to harden, how to paint it, and what to do about cracks. White takes soft colours best. A 500 g block is enough for a ring dish and a trinket dish, and for one small tray as well if you keep everything small. Buy a second block if you want room to start over. Check the maker's own instructions before you start, because drying times and thickness advice vary by brand.

**Paint.** Ordinary acrylic paint, in small tubes, does the job. Choose a set that has a few soft shades (blush, butter yellow, a sage or olive green), plus white and one strong accent, like a cherry red. A twelve-colour set is plenty for three small pieces. Clay makers such as Mont Marte say you can decorate with acrylic paint once the piece has dried completely.

**A finish (optional).** A matte, water-based clay sealer keeps the surface looking natural and adds a protective layer. It is a nice extra, not a necessity, and it does not turn your dish into tableware. Crayola says sealing will not waterproof clay, so keep every piece dry either way. Brush it on with a soft, wide synthetic brush, in a room with an open window, and try it on a spare scrap first, because the maker's instructions for your clay are the ones to trust.

**From the kitchen drawer:**

- A rolling pin, or a straight-sided bottle
- A butter knife and a toothpick
- A lid, a small bowl or a paper template to cut around
- Baking parchment, or cling film, to roll on
- A small bowl of water and a sponge
- Fine sandpaper
- A soft paintbrush for colour

A scalloped cutter is a pleasant extra, but a paper template and a butter knife make the same shape.

## Three beginner projects

Before you start, one rule: **keep the thickness even.** Makers give different minimums. Mont Marte's tray project rolls the clay to 1 cm, Crayola says at least 1/4 inch, and Sculpd says at least 1/2 inch in all places. Follow the maker of the clay you buy, and aim for the same thickness all over. Makers also say that too much water and uneven drying are what cause cracks.

### 1. The pinch-pot ring dish (no tools)

1. Roll a ball of clay about the size of a small plum, in your palms.
2. Press your thumb into the middle, stopping before you go through the bottom.
3. Pinch slowly around the ball, turning it as you go, until the walls feel even and the dish is shallow.
4. Smooth any cracks with a fingertip that is barely damp.
5. Set it on parchment to dry.

Paint it white, then dab a cherry-red rim on the edge once it is dry.

### 2. The scalloped trinket dish

1. Roll the clay out between two sheets of cling film, to an even thickness.
2. Cut a circle around a lid or bowl. For a scalloped edge, press a small round lid or the rim of a bottle cap into the edge, overlapping the dents all the way round, or cut a bow or heart from a paper template.
3. Lift the shape and pinch or roll the edge upward to form a shallow curve.
4. Smooth the edge with a damp fingertip and leave it to dry.

Pastel colours suit this one: blush with a butter-yellow rim.

### 3. The little tray

1. Roll about 500 g, in a single block, to an even thickness.
2. Cut an oval, roughly 22 x 12 cm, around a paper template.
3. Score a line about 1 cm inside the edge. Roll a thin coil of clay and press it onto the line to make a low lip, then blend the join with a damp fingertip.
4. Press a pattern in with a toothpick or the back of a spoon.
5. Leave it to dry.

This is the same shape as Mont Marte's own trinket-dish project, which is a good place to see how it goes before you start.

## Drying, painting and finishing

Do not hurry the drying. Put each piece somewhere cool and quiet, away from radiators and direct sun, and leave it alone. Do not use the oven, because Sculpd says not to apply heat to its clay.

Makers' own guidance on time:

- **Mont Marte:** about 24 hours to reach earthenware hardness, and longer for thicker pieces. For its tray project, it says to let the piece dry for 24 to 48 hours before painting.
- **Crayola:** about 24 hours dry to the touch and 72 hours to dry completely. It says conditions and size change this.
- **Sculpd:** 24 to 48 hours, longer for thicker pieces.

So the honest answer is "a day or two, perhaps longer", and the clay you buy will tell you more. Once a piece is completely dry, lightly sand any rough edge, then paint it. If you want to seal it, Mont Marte advises waiting at least 24 to 72 hours after painting, then brushing on two or three even coats. Keep the room ventilated.

## When it goes a bit wrong

- **A crack.** Press a little fresh clay into it, smooth it with a barely damp finger, and let it dry slowly. Uneven thickness, too much water and drying too fast are the usual causes, according to the makers.
- **A rough edge.** Wait until it is completely dry, then sand it lightly. Wipe off the dust before you paint.
- **Tacky paint.** Thick paint takes longer to dry than you would expect. Leave it for a day, or apply thinner coats next time. If you have sealed it, wait for the paint to be fully dry first.

## Questions people ask

**Do I need an oven or a kiln?**
No. Air-dry clay hardens in the air. Sculpd specifically says not to put its clay in the oven.

**What can I put in it?**
Rings, earrings and keys. Not water: Sculpd says its clay is porous and can absorb water even after drying. Mont Marte also states plainly that air-dry clay is not food-safe, so keep it away from anything you will eat.

**Do I have to seal it?**
No. A finish helps protect the surface and the paint, but it is not essential, and it will not make the clay waterproof or food-safe.

## Your next step

Pick up a block of white clay and a small set of acrylics this week. Make the ring dish on Sunday, leave it on the windowsill, and paint it when it is dry. By midweek you will have something small, pretty and entirely yours.
