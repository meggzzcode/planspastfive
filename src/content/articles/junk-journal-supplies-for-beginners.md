Picture the page. A cream envelope tucked under a ticket stub, one strip of tape in a colour that makes you smile, a line of your own handwriting underneath. Turn it over and the next page is just as full. The book has swollen a little, the corners have gone soft, and it looks like something that has been loved for a year, although it has only been a month.

That is a junk journal, and it takes four things to begin: a sturdy book, something to stick with, a pen and a roll of tape. Choose a mood first, buy a few things that agree with each other, and your first page feels like the start of a collection.

## Choose your look

A book that sticks to one palette looks designed, even when it is chaos underneath. Decide on a mood before you buy any tape.

**Shoebox Post.** Warm and well travelled. A tan or kraft book, black ink, tape in black, silver or gold.
- *Collect:* airmail envelopes, postmarks, stamps, boarding passes, the receipt from a very good lunch.
- *Tape:* black, or black and gold dots. One gold strip across a corner is plenty.
- *Finishing touch:* kraft tags and type-style captions.

**Garden Press.** A pressed-flower book. Cream pages, a lot of green and a little sunshine.
- *Collect:* seed packets, plant tags, catalogue pages, small botanical prints, a pressed leaf or two.
- *Tape:* butter yellow and leaf green.
- *Finishing touch:* a sheet of illustrated flower stickers, which does the work of a pot of paint.

**Tea Rose.** Soft, romantic and a little old-fashioned. Pale cream pages and a lot of blush.
- *Collect:* love-letter paper, old valentines, floral wrapping, tissue paper, a ribbon end or a scrap of lace.
- *Tape:* dusty pink and blush, with perhaps one narrow gold line.
- *Finishing touch:* a pressed rose, and ink kept soft in brown or black, never bright.

## The four things to buy

The short list: a heavy-paper book, dry adhesive, a pigment-ink pen and a small set of tape. Scissors you probably own. Everything else is paper.

### 1. A book that can take glue

Layered paper and adhesive make a page swell, and a thin writing notebook ripples like a crisp packet. Look for:

- **Heavy paper.** Mixed-media or heavyweight sketch paper rather than ordinary notebook paper. The weight is printed on the cover or in the listing; the higher, the better for layering.
- **A binding that lies open.** Sewn bindings tend to sit flatter than glued ones.
- **A toned or cream page.** A tan page gives you a background colour before you start, so a sparse spread looks deliberate.
- **A modest size.** Around A5, roughly a paperback. A small book fills fast enough to feel like progress.

Tan pages suit Shoebox Post and Garden Press; a pale cream page lets Tea Rose glow. Expect the book to be your biggest purchase, and the first thing you run out of. That is a good problem.

### 2. Something to stick with

Tombow lists junk journaling among the uses of this applicator and describes the adhesive as acid-free. Those are the maker's statements. The refill is a separate purchase, so it is worth lining up each piece before it touches the page.

### 3. One good black pen

Captions and dates are what turn a collage into a diary. Look for a **pigment-ink fineliner** in black, ideally with a fine nib for small notes and a slightly bolder one for headings. Check the label for waterproof or fade-resistant if you plan to paint or stamp over your writing, and try it on paper before a glossy sticker, because many pens skip on shiny surfaces. Shoebox Post wants true black; Garden Press and Tea Rose look prettier with a fine nib and plenty of white space around the handwriting.

### 4. Tape that sets the colour

One roll of washi goes a long way. Choose a **15 mm roll or a small coordinated set** in your palette and try a strip on a scrap before using it on a good page, because adhesion varies by brand and paper. Tape from a named maker is easier to trust than an unbranded bag of forty rolls, and three colours that agree beat a rainbow that argues. Mixing is allowed, but give each book one lead direction and borrow a single touch from another, such as a gold strip in a rose book.

You will also want any small, sharp scissors. Wipe the adhesive off the blades afterwards.

## Paper worth saving

The raw material is usually already in the recycling:

- Envelopes, especially airmail and window ones
- Ticket stubs, receipts, boarding passes
- Tea-bag wrappers and sugar sachets
- A page from a paperback you do not mind cutting
- Wrapping paper offcuts, shop bags, stamps

Keep it all in an envelope for a week and you will have a page's worth by Friday.

## Your first spread, in seven steps

Set a timer for 45 minutes and treat it as a warm-up.

1. **Open the book flat** and press the middle with your palm.
2. **Lay out before you stick.** Put five or six pieces down loosely: one large, two medium, three small.
3. **Overlap, do not float.** Let pieces tuck under or over one another so the page holds together.
4. **Stick the biggest first,** then work down in size, lining each piece up before it touches the page.
5. **Add one strip of tape** across a corner or an edge. One. Step back.
6. **Write something small:** a date and a sentence about where the ticket came from. A caption, not a diary entry.
7. **Close the book** and leave it under a stack of cookbooks overnight.

It will be a little lopsided, which is the style. For layouts to copy, try [Your First Junk Journal Spread](/journal/your-first-junk-journal-spread/).

## Questions beginners ask

**Do I need real vintage paper?** No. Modern receipts, packaging and printed napkins look wonderful. Old paper is a mood, not a requirement.

**Can I use my own photos?** Yes. Use copies of anything irreplaceable. Makers describe some adhesives as photo-safe, but that is their statement, and a conservator is the right source for precious prints.

**What if I hate my page?** Cover it. A bigger piece of paper, a strip of tape and a new caption turn a page you dislike into a layer.
