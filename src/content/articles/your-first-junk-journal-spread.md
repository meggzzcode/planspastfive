It is 5:40 on a Tuesday. The laptop is shut, the kettle is on, and there is a drawer in the kitchen full of things you could not bring yourself to throw away: a cinema ticket, a postcard from a friend, the label off a very good jam. That drawer is your first spread, waiting.

A junk journal is a notebook you fill with small paper things, layered and stuck down however you like. Nobody grades it. This plan makes one spread, which is two facing pages, in about an hour.

## Gather, then stop gathering

Pull out what you already have and keep it to a handful of piles. Aim for about a dozen pieces in all.

- Anything with writing on it: tickets, receipts, a handwritten recipe card.
- Anything with a picture: stamps, magazine corners, a photograph you have two copies of.
- Anything with texture: a paper bag, tissue, a page from an old book, a pressed flower.
- Something with a colour you love, which will become the thread running through the page.

> The page does not need to be good. It needs to be yours, and finished by the time the tea goes cold.

Choose a book to fill. Any sturdy notebook works, and a hardback holds up best once the pages get thick with glue and paper.

## Lay it out before you stick anything

This is the step that separates a pleasant evening from a frustrating one. Arrange everything on the open pages with nothing attached. Put the largest piece slightly off centre, overlap the edges of two or three more, and tuck a small thing where two pieces meet. Step back. Move one piece. Stop when you smile at it.

Take a quick photo of the arrangement on your phone, so you can rebuild it if the dog walks through.

## Stick it down, flat

Here is where most first spreads go wrong. Wet glue wrinkles thin paper, and a wrinkled ticket looks tired. A dry adhesive runner solves it. Work from the bottom layer up, one piece at a time. Press each one down with the flat of your thumb for a few seconds.

Already own a glue stick? Use it. Thin paper will wrinkle a little more, and some people find that part of the charm.

## Finish with one handwritten line

Add a single line in pen. A date, a place, a small joy: "Tuesday. Jam on toast. Better things are coming." Leave the rest of the white space alone. A page that breathes looks more finished than one filled to the edges.

Close the book and leave it open on the side table for a day, so anything curling has time to settle.
