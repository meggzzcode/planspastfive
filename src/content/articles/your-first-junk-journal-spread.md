It is 5:40 on a Tuesday. The laptop is shut, the kettle is on, and somewhere in the kitchen there is a drawer you have been politely ignoring. It holds a cinema ticket, a postcard from a friend, the label off a genuinely excellent jam, and four takeaway menus. Congratulations. That drawer is your first spread.

A junk journal is a notebook you fill with small paper things, layered and stuck down however you like. Nobody marks it. This plan makes one spread (two facing pages) in about an hour, with tea.

## Raid the drawer, then stop

Pull out what you already own and sort it into piles. A dozen pieces is plenty. More than that and you are not journaling, you are archiving.

- Things with writing on them: tickets, receipts, a handwritten recipe card.
- Things with a picture: stamps, magazine corners, the second copy of a photo.
- Things with texture: a paper bag, tissue, a page from a book that was already falling apart, a pressed flower.
- One thing in a colour you love. It becomes the thread through the page.

> The page does not need to be good. It needs to be yours, and finished before the tea goes cold.

Pick a notebook. A hardback is happiest here, since pages swell once they are loaded with paper and glue.

## Lay it out before you stick anything

This is the step that separates a pleasant evening from a sulk. Arrange everything on the open pages with nothing attached. Put the biggest piece slightly off centre, overlap the edges of two or three more, and tuck something small where two pieces meet. Step back. Squint. Move one piece. Stop when you smile at it.

Take a quick photo of the layout, so you can rebuild it after the cat walks across it.

## Stick it down, flat

Here is where first spreads usually come unstuck. Wet glue wrinkles thin paper, and a wrinkled ticket looks like it has had a long week. A dry adhesive runner fixes that. Work from the bottom layer up, one piece at a time, and press each one down with your thumb for a few seconds.

Already own a glue stick? Use it. Thin paper will wrinkle a little more, and plenty of people decide that is the charm.

## Finish with one line, in pen

Write a single line: a date, a place, a small joy. "Tuesday. Jam on toast. Better things are coming." Leave the rest of the white space alone. A page that breathes looks more finished than one filled to the edges.

Close the book and leave it open on the side table overnight, so anything curling has time to calm down.

Then do nothing. Look at the page on Thursday and see what the next one wants.
